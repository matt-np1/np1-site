Indicative and confidential. The offer is proposed, and its terms are subject to legal and accounting review before any agreement.
01

Summary and the offer

Uniformitys is raising R5m to build a uniform factory and shop in Kabokweni, Mpumalanga. Four schools are fully on board and it has sold to a handful of others, but it can serve only about 10% of their learners, because bought-in stock from Johannesburg takes up to three months. The next 5 or 6 schools are ready for a full ramp-up, and 23 schools in the area have expressed real intent to make Uniformitys their supplier. With local production, each school is worth about R1m per year, and the business turns cash positive from 2028.

What, proof, opportunity

WhatUniformitys provides school uniforms where parents live and work in townships, moving towards local manufacture and bringing pride to kasi schools.

OpportunityNo one supplies these schools locally. Parents travel up to 40 km by taxi to town to buy generic uniforms and try to match colours and badges themselves. Existing schools have never had a local supplier, and new schools open every year.

Sphiwe with uniform samples, Kabokweni.

The problem

  1. Most schools around Kabokweni have no uniform supplier at all. Many had no formal uniform until recently, and hardly any have full uniform adoption. Parents want uniforms and will pay for them: this is an availability problem, not a demand problem.
  2. Local options are a few informal traders and home sewers, with uneven quality and no school branding.
  3. Uniformitys started as sourcing and distribution. Bought-in stock sits at the bottom of Johannesburg makers’ queues, with waits of three months or more, reaching only about 10% of learners at the schools it serves.
  4. The founder grew up with this. Sphiwe Masinga, 30, started building Uniformitys in 2024 while working in a call centre, and went full time on 1 January 2025.
03

How Uniformitys works

Uniformitys signs up a school, agrees its uniform list and specification, then sells direct to that school’s families. The school pays no deposit and carries no cash-flow risk, which is why schools commit.

StepWhat happens
1. Agree the schoolSphiwe works with the principal or uniform committee on design, colours and crest. Emtfonjeni Secondary has signed a letter of intent.
2. Sell to familiesParents order through WhatsApp, at pop-up stalls on school pickup days, or at the counter. They pay by card, payment link, cash or lay-by.
3. MakeCut, sew, brand and finish. Today 3 tailors on 3 machines make about 40% of garments; the rest is bought in from Johannesburg.
4. DeliverCollection at the workshop or delivery to the school at the start of term.

07

The offer

R5m to build the factory and shop in Kabokweni: a R3m secured note and R2m of equity for 20%.

  1. Minimum ticket and minimum close. Minimum ticket R250k, in the note or equity. The minimum close is R1.5m, which the note covers.
  2. What each tranche funds. R500k stocks the 2027 season; R1.5m opens the premises and shop with stock; R5m adds the production divisions and the team.
  3. How the money comes back. The note is repaid in equal instalments from 2028 to 2032 after one year of capitalised interest. Equity returns through dividends from 2030, paid from half of the cash above R1m, and through the value of the stake.

Why this structure

  1. Most of the money buys hard assets: R863k of property and R2.23m of equipment, which suits a secured note at a cost the business can carry from 2028.
  2. The note is sized to the floor case: even at R750k per school the business is EBITDA-positive from 2028 and repays the note in full, using a one-off capital holiday if needed.
  3. Equity takes the production ramp risk and shares in the upside: each school above the base adds about R450k of contribution a year.

Uniformitys is open to an investor’s own term sheet.
